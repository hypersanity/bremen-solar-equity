# Bremen's solar map is a map of houses

*Residential rooftop and plug-in photovoltaics (PV) per household, by postcode, in the Land Bremen (the city of Bremen and Bremerhaven), November 2026*

---

**Blunt summary.** Whether a household in Bremen has solar power depends first on whether it lives in a house. Postcodes made up of one- and two-family houses have up to 61 times more rooftop PV per household than the inner-city postcodes of flats. Income works as a second, smaller filter: among houses, those in rich postcodes have rooftop PV three to four times as often as those in poor ones. Plug-in "balcony" PV, the route that federal law now opens to tenants, follows the housing stock even more closely than rooftop PV does. The two legal models for supplying tenants with electricity from their building's roof have barely been used. Bremen's one programme for low-income tenants was voted through by the state parliament in 2022, and the Senate, Bremen's state government, reported in December 2023 that it had not started.

---

## 1. The question and its limits

Housing decides a household's access to solar electricity in Bremen, before income does. This study tests that claim in three steps. Part I sets out what a household faces: the cost, the tax and fee rules, the paperwork, the grid connection and the payback against grid electricity. Part II measures where residential solar actually is, postcode by postcode. Part III explains the gaps from documented evidence and marks every inference that goes beyond it.

The study covers residential access only. It excludes commercial, public, industrial, agricultural and ground-mounted systems. It covers the whole Land, including Bremerhaven, about 50 km north of Bremen. Many Bremen statistics leave Bremerhaven out, even though its owner-occupancy rate is lower: 30.1 % against 37.0 % in the city of Bremen.[58]

Two terms recur throughout. **Rooftop PV** means a building-mounted PV system registered as serving a household. **Plug-in PV** means a small "balcony" system that plugs into a socket. The two are different products with different buyers, so the study reports them separately.

## 2. Part I: what decides a household's access

### 2.1 The price of entry

A rooftop system costs five figures and a plug-in system costs three. The consumer advice service Verbraucherzentrale Nordrhein-Westfalen analysed 71 real offers made between January 2025 and August 2026.[1] Its index shows the price per kilowatt-peak (kWp) falling steeply as systems get bigger:

| System size (kWp) | 4–6 | >6–8 | >8–9 | >9–10 | >10–12 | >12–14 | >14–16 | >16 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Average turnkey price without battery, € per kWp | €2,090 | €1,820 | €1,520 | €1,410 | €1,290 | €1,240 | €1,220 | €1,010 |

*Source: Verbraucherzentrale price index, as of 28 August 2026.[1]*

The research institute Fraunhofer ISE gives a wider band of €700 to €2,000 per kWp and reports that small systems have become more expensive.[2] A battery adds €360 to €510 per kWh of storage in the Verbraucherzentrale index.[1] A plug-in device with a standard module costs between €200 and €600.[3]

The smallest roofs pay the highest price per kWp. That penalty falls on terraced housing, which is the commonest building type in the city of Bremen (section 5.2).

### 2.2 Tax and fees

The federal government removed the main tax costs for small systems from 2023. Supply and installation of PV on or near a dwelling carry no VAT if the system is 30 kWp or smaller (§12(3) UStG),[4] and plug-in devices have also carried no VAT since 1 January 2023.[3] Income from building-mounted PV up to 30 kWp per dwelling is exempt from income tax (§3 No. 72 EStG).[5] The law caps the annual cost of a smart meter at €130 for a system of 7 to 15 kW, and at €60 for a meter the household installs voluntarily (§30 MsbG).[6]

Each of these rules lowers the cost of owning PV. None of them helps a household that has no roof to put it on.

### 2.3 Paperwork and the grid connection

A private household cannot register its own rooftop system with the grid operator; only an installer can. The grid operator for both cities is wesernetz, which runs wesernetz Bremen GmbH and wesernetz Bremerhaven GmbH under one management.[32] Federal law requires grid operators to connect renewable plants without delay and to run a web portal for connection requests (§8 EEG 2023).[7] wesernetz requires an installer to register every rooftop system with it before installation.[8] The review is free for systems up to 30 kW.[8] The owner must then enter the system in the federal register of energy plants, the *Marktstammdatenregister* (the register), within one month of switching it on.[8]

In December 2024 the *Weser-Kurier* reported that wesernetz could connect every new system but could not say when, with some waits longer than six months.[36]

Federal law treats plug-in PV far more lightly. A plug-in device is a legal category of up to 800 W at the inverter and 2,000 W of modules.[16] Its owner no longer has to notify the grid operator, and registration in the register now asks for five technical details.[17] A tenant can demand the landlord's consent to the changes a plug-in device needs (§554 BGB),[18] and a flat owner has the same right against the owners' association (§20 WEG).[19]

wesernetz contradicts that federal rule on its own website. Its information page for generators, read on 10 November 2026, says plug-in devices "must be registered with the grid operator".[37] *Hypothesis, untested here:* outdated instructions do most to deter the households that have no installer to ask.

### 2.4 Feed-in payments and the 2027 bill

A household that installed rooftop PV of up to 10 kW between 1 August and 31 December 2026 receives 7.70 cents for each kilowatt-hour it exports, or 12.22 cents if it exports everything.[9] Since February 2025 the payment falls to zero in hours of negative wholesale prices (§51 EEG 2023),[10] and a system under 25 kW without a smart meter and control box may feed in at most 60 % of its rated power (§9 EEG 2023).[11]

The tariff itself is about to change. The Bundesnetzagentur, the federal grid regulator, notes that the current law runs to 31 December 2026 and that its successor is before parliament.[9] The government's bill (Bundestag document 21/7867) abolishes the fixed tariff for new plants, largely ends support for new plants under 25 kW and exempts plug-in devices up to 2 kW from the new feed-in limits.[12] Plants switched on before 1 January 2027 keep the old terms.[12] The Bundestag, the federal parliament, held the first reading on 24 September 2026, and the Bundesrat, the chamber of the states, opposed ending the rooftop tariff.[13] The trade journal *pv magazine* expected the final vote in November 2026.[38] On 10 November 2026 the bill had not passed.

The cut-off has a beneficiary. Households that install before 2027 keep a fixed tariff for 20 years, and Part II shows that those households live mainly in owner-occupied suburbs. A later adopter in a poorer postcode would earn only the value of the electricity it uses itself.

### 2.5 Two legal routes for tenants

German law offers landlords two ways to sell roof electricity to their tenants. Under the tenant-electricity model, *Mieterstrom*, the price may be at most 90 % of the local basic tariff, and the supply contract must be separate from the lease (§42a EnWG).[14] The model attracts a small bonus of 2.51 cents per kWh for systems switched on in late 2026.[9] The newer shared building supply, introduced by the 2024 solar package, frees the operator from supplying the tenants' remaining demand but requires every participant's consumption to be metered every quarter hour (§42b EnWG).[15] Section 5.5 shows how little either has been used.

### 2.7 The grid by neighbourhood

No public source shows how much solar capacity each neighbourhood's grid can take. wesernetz states that there is no congestion in either city's network.[33] The federal rules for the grid operators' joint platform require them to publish connection procedures, regional scenarios and network expansion plans, but not a capacity map (§14e EnWG).[34] wesernetz's page on that platform links to its connection portal and its expansion plan.[35] Part II therefore cannot test grid capacity as a cause. The one documented friction on the grid side is delay.[36]

### 2.8 Payback against the grid

Over 20 years rooftop PV beats grid electricity easily; over the first five it is a large debt. The energy industry association BDEW puts the average household electricity price in 2026 at 37.3 cents per kWh.[29] Fraunhofer ISE estimates that small rooftop systems generate electricity at 6 to 14 cents per kWh, that households use 20 to 40 % of their own output without a battery, and that a battery typically doubles that share.[30]

The illustration below uses two assumptions of mine. I assume a yield of 900 kWh per kWp a year, below Fraunhofer ISE's figure of about 1,080 for a new plant facing due south,[30] because Bremen's roofs face every direction. I ignore maintenance, ageing, discounting and lost hours of feed-in. Formula: annual value = generation × self-use share × grid price + generation × (1 − self-use share) × feed-in tariff; payback = cost ÷ annual value (script `10_economics.py`).

**Rooftop PV, 8 kWp at €1,820 per kWp, total €14,560:**

| Scenario | Self-use share | Feed-in tariff | Value per year | Simple payback |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Base | 30 % | 7.70 c | €1,194 | 12.2 years |
| More self-use (battery or habits) | 40 % | 7.70 c | €1,407 | 10.3 years |
| No tariff, as under the 2027 bill | 30 % | 0 c | €806 | 18.1 years |
| Yield of 800 kWh per kWp | 30 % | 7.70 c | €1,061 | 13.7 years |
| Grid price of 30 c | 30 % | 7.70 c | €1,036 | 14.1 years |

The payback is the same in every postcode, but the outlay is not the same burden. That €14,560 equals 88 % of the median taxable income per taxpayer in Gröpelingen (postcode 28237, €16,500 in 2020) and 33 % in Oberneuland (28355, €44,300). Those ratios are my calculation from the incomes in the *Ortsteilatlas*, the state statistics office's atlas of the city's sub-districts (section 3.2).

**Plug-in PV, an 800 W device at €400 to €600.** With my assumed yields of 500 to 700 kWh per kWp and self-use of 40 to 70 %, a device saves €60 to €146 a year and pays back in 2.7 to 10.1 years. The Verbraucherzentrale Schleswig-Holstein gives one worked example: a 500 W device for €450 "could" pay back in six to eight years.[3] Plug-in PV is cheap to start, but it covers a small part of a household's demand and earns nothing for exports.

### 3.3 What counts as residential

The register classifies each solar system by type and by the main use of the building it sits on. I fixed both outcome definitions before looking at any postcode figure. Rooftop PV is a building-mounted system (`Gebäudesolaranlage`) on a building whose use is "household" (`Haushalt`) and which is in operation. Plug-in PV is a system of the plug-in type (`Steckerfertige Solaranlage`) in operation.

| Filter step (script `06_analysis.py`) | Systems |
|---|---|
| All solar systems with a municipality code of Bremen or Bremerhaven | 20,300 |
| In operation | 19,438 |
| Building-mounted | 11,118 |
| Of which building use "household": **rooftop PV** | **9,402** |
| Postcode inside the Land boundary | 9,401 |
| Plug-in systems in operation: **plug-in PV** | **8,313** |

The filter removed building-mounted systems on commercial buildings (823), on buildings marked "other" (210), on public buildings (187), in industry (65) and in farming (49), plus 382 with no use recorded. It also removed seven ground-mounted systems.

### 3.4 Spatial processing

I assigned each inhabited 100 m cell of the census grid to the postcode that contains its centre point, after clipping the postcodes to the Land boundary. All 12,662 inhabited cells inside the Land fell into a postcode. I summed counts per postcode and rebuilt rates from the summed counts.

The *Ortsteilatlas* stores its boundaries as compressed pixel paths, not as a map file. I decoded them into 88 *Ortsteil* polygons and compared them with the OpenStreetMap outlines of the city districts. The overlap score (intersection over union) was 0.96 to 0.995 for 21 of 22 districts and 0.74 for the port district. Each census-grid cell takes its *Ortsteil*'s income and SGB II share, and each postcode takes the population-weighted mean of its cells.

The statistics are Spearman rank correlations (40 postcodes; 33 where income exists), least-squares regressions on standardised variables with robust (HC3) standard errors, Gini coefficients and concentration indices weighted by households, and Moran's I for spatial clustering with queen contiguity and 9,999 permutations.

### 3.6 Where the method is weak

My choice of outcome understates access in flats. A single rooftop system on a block can supply many flats through *Mieterstrom* or shared supply, yet the register counts it once. Measuring capacity per household instead gives almost the same ranking (rank correlation 0.96) and a Gini of 0.44, so the bias does not reverse the result, but it does exist and it works against the inner city.

The households date from May 2022 and the solar systems from November 2026. Each postcode holds 5,000 to 15,000 households, so differences inside a postcode disappear. Income (2020) and the SGB II share (2024) are *Ortsteil* figures carried onto postcodes. The census suppresses or blurs some small cells for confidentiality, although the totals match the official figures. The building use "household" is whatever the owner entered at registration. Correlations here describe patterns, not causes, and Part III separates the two.

| Rooftop PV ~ owner-occupancy + income (city of Bremen) | 33 | 0.75\*\*\* | 0.32 (p = 0.035) | 0.80 |
| Plug-in PV ~ house share + income (city of Bremen) | 33 | 0.95\*\*\* | −0.02 (p = 0.83) | 0.90 |
| Rooftop PV per 100 one- and two-family buildings ~ income (city of Bremen) | 33 | n/a | 0.64\*\*\* | 0.41 |
| Rooftop PV ~ house share + Bremerhaven indicator (Land) | 40 | 0.92\*\*\* | Bremerhaven +0.23\*\*\* | 0.84 |

House share is the first filter. Once it is known, the regressions account for 88 to 90 % of the differences between postcodes in both rooftop PV and plug-in PV.

Income is the second filter, and it works inside the house stock. Dividing rooftop systems by the number of one- and two-family buildings holds housing roughly constant, and income then explains 41 % of what is left. A house in Oberneuland (15.4 systems per 100 houses) or Horn-Lehe and Borgfeld (13.9) is three to four times as likely to have rooftop PV as a house in Gröpelingen (4.1 and 4.8), Walle (2.8 and 3.7) or Hemelingen's 28207 (3.6). The SGB II share points the same way, with a correlation of −0.45.

Plug-in PV shows no income effect at all, and it follows house share more tightly than rooftop PV does. In the most-rented postcodes it makes up most of the residential solar there is: 71 % of systems in Mitte (28195) and Walle (28217), and 73 % in Hemelingen (28207). The plug-in share of a postcode's systems falls as owner-occupancy rises (rank correlation −0.59, p < 0.001). In absolute terms the most-rented fifth of households still has only 8.8 plug-in systems per 1,000, against 40 to 60 on the suburban edge.

The literature on peer effects predicts that solar clusters in space beyond what housing and income explain. At postcode scale Bremen shows no such clustering: Moran's I is 0.11 for rooftop PV (p = 0.10), 0.13 for plug-in PV (p = 0.09) and 0.12 for the regression residuals (p = 0.11). Section 5.4 discusses what this does and does not rule out.

### 4.5 Bremerhaven against Bremen

Bremerhaven has the lower owner-occupancy rate[58] and the higher rate of rooftop PV: 30.5 systems per 1,000 households against 26.3, and still 0.23 standard deviations higher once house share is controlled for. The commissioning years locate the difference (`city_yearly.json`):

| Rooftop PV commissioned per 1,000 households | 2022 | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 | 2026 (to 8 November) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| City of Bremen | 1.4 | 4.2 | 6.1 | **5.2** | 4.4 |
| Bremerhaven | 1.7 | 5.0 | **8.4** | 4.1 | 3.9 |

Bremerhaven's lead comes from 2023 and 2024, the years of its 25 % grant for private PV, which opened in January 2023 and has since ended.[22] In 2025 Bremerhaven falls behind Bremen. *This is an inference from timing, not a causal estimate:* Bremerhaven also started from a larger stock before 2023 (9.2 systems per 1,000 households against 6.3). The grant excluded plug-in devices,[23] and Bremerhaven's plug-in rate is the lower of the two (21.0 against 24.5).

In December 2023 the Senate stated that solar was growing "much faster" in the city of Bremen than in Bremerhaven.[70] That statement measured total capacity, commercial systems included. Per household and for homes only, the register shows the reverse for 2023 and 2024.

## 5. Part III: why the gaps exist

I mark each explanation below as **documented** (direct evidence), **consistent** (Bremen matches findings from elsewhere) or **hypothesis** (reasoned, not tested).

### 5.1 Owner-occupancy: documented

The steepest gradient in Bremen runs along owner-occupancy: eight times as much rooftop PV in the most owner-occupied fifth as in the most rented. The American and German research reaches the same conclusion by other routes. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory finds that US rooftop adopters earn 53 % more than the median household, but only 23 % more than the median owner-occupier.[39] Owner-occupancy absorbs most of what looks like an income gap. In the US, 97 % of systems on owner-occupied homes in 2023 were on detached houses, and homes occupied by tenants took about 4 % of all installations.[39] A German study of household survey data restricts its sample to owners "because only owners can decide", and it drops residents of high-rise buildings, for whom a system of their own is "highly unlikely".[43]

Bremen's housing makes this filter harder than the German average. The owner-occupancy rate is 37.0 % in the city of Bremen and 30.1 % in Bremerhaven,[58] against 44 % for Germany in the same census.[54] The federal statistics office's household survey puts the Land's owner-occupancy rate at 32.0 %.[60] In the Land, 62.4 % of dwellings are rented, 24.3 % belong to owners' associations, which must vote on any roof installation, and 14.4 % belong to the municipality or its companies.[59]

### 5.2 Built form: documented in the data

House share predicts solar better than owner-occupancy does, because the owner of a house decides about a roof that no neighbour shares. Bremen built its 19th-century expansion as terraced family houses, the *Bremer Haus*, in place of tenements.[61] As a result, 57 % of the residential buildings in the city of Bremen are terraced: 69,448 of 122,273.[58] The Land's climate agency, energiekonsens, contrasts the typical *Bremer Haus* with the large roofs of many blocks of flats.[57] The smallest systems cost the most per kWp: €2,090 at 4 to 6 kWp against €1,010 above 16 kWp.[1]

Roof area does not limit the city as a whole. The 2020 solar roof survey found about 10 million m² of well-suited roof in the city of Bremen, enough in theory for about 1,500 MW, while 36 MW was connected.[74]

*Hypothesis:* small terraced roofs, a high price per kWp and listed-building rules keep prosperous Schwachhausen at 12 to 27 systems per 1,000 households. A test would need roof-level data from the solar roof survey, which this study did not use.

### 5.3 Income and upfront capital: consistent

Poverty in Bremen has an address. About one resident in four in Gröpelingen lives on *Bürgergeld*, the basic income benefit.[62] At the end of 2022 the share of residents under pension age in SGB II households ran from 4.2 % in Oberneuland to 32.9 % in Gröpelingen.[63] The Senate's social monitoring places most high-status *Ortsteile* in the east of the city and the low-status ones in the west.[64] That is close to the map of rooftop PV in Fig. 1, which is why this study needs a measure that holds housing constant before it can say anything about income.

With housing held constant, richer postcodes still convert houses to solar three to four times as often (section 4.4). Research in Flanders found the same "strong unconditional income effect", working mostly through ownership and suitable houses.[44] A German study of household survey data found investment in PV "mainly driven by the economic factors".[43]

Part I points to cash, not return, as the mechanism. A €14,560 system pays back in about 12 years in Gröpelingen and in Oberneuland alike, but it costs 88 % of a median taxpayer's annual income in the first and 33 % in the second. Bremen's support does not bridge that difference: its main instrument is a loan for owners with credit,[24] and no grant depends on income (section 2.6).

The flow of money through the old feed-in system ran the same way. An analysis of German household survey data from 2010 to 2017 found the cost of the renewable surcharge spread "almost evenly", and therefore regressively, while the feed-in payments went increasingly to higher-income owners of PV.[48] Earlier studies reached the same verdict.[49][50] In 2012, households at risk of poverty spent on average 5.5 % of their income on electricity.[51]

### 5.4 Peer effects: predicted by the literature, absent at postcode scale

Research elsewhere finds that people install solar when their neighbours have. In Connecticut, one more system within half a mile in the previous year raised later installations measurably.[41] A study of 576,000 German household systems found that "imitative adoption behavior is highly localized".[42] Sunter and colleagues argue that disadvantaged neighbourhoods in the US stay behind because they lack the first few systems that start the process.[40]

Bremen's postcodes show no significant clustering once housing and income are known (section 4.4). *Hypothesis:* the imitation happens street by street and averages out across postcodes of 5,000 to 15,000 households. The register's confidentiality rule for systems of 30 kWp or less[75] keeps that hypothesis out of reach of public data.

### 5.5 The tenant routes: documented failure

Both tenant routes have failed nationally, not only in Bremen. The federal government's own review in 2019 counted 677 *Mieterstrom* systems with 13.9 MW in total and judged the framework inadequate.[52] By May 2024 the national count was just under 9,000; the economic research institute IW Köln blames the lack of common metering and billing standards across more than 900 grid operators.[53] A 2025 analysis for the Ariadne research project found that recent reforms have produced no noticeable increase in PV on blocks of flats.[54] DIW Berlin, another economic research institute, describes the model as having played "practically no role".[55]

In Bremen, energiekonsens reports that most *Mieterstrom* providers turn down buildings with fewer than 20 flats and that only one local provider is known.[57] It adds that hardly any property manager or owners' association can carry the metering and administration alone.[57]

The housing companies that own the roofs in the lowest-solar postcodes have moved slowly. GEWOBA, the city's largest landlord, runs 62 systems with about 2,000 kWp and plans 152 by 2029.[68] In its contribution to a Senate report, GEWOBA states that Bremen's building code bars PV from its high-rise blocks on fire-safety grounds.[70] BREBAU, another Bremen housing company, supplies 36 flats from its first *Mieterstrom* project.[71] The most-rented fifth alone holds about 50,000 renting households.

The one instrument aimed at low-income tenants did not arrive. The state parliament asked for a plug-in grant for tenants on benefits in 2022.[25] In December 2023 the Senate reported no programme and no applications, and it cited budget rules that make a full grant for benefit recipients difficult.[26] An NGO, Bremer SolidarStrom, hands out devices on a small scale in its place.[27]

The policy framing of plug-in PV as the tenants' route predicts the highest plug-in rates where renting is commonest. Bremen shows the opposite (section 4.4), and the user surveys agree. HTW Berlin found plug-in users to be mostly men over 45 with higher incomes who own their homes; the idea that plug-in PV appeals only to tenants "could not be confirmed".[46] An earlier survey found 80 % of users were homeowners.[47]

### 5.6 Paperwork: documented friction, size unknown

Three frictions are on record. Only an installer can register rooftop PV with wesernetz.[8] Connection waits have run beyond six months.[36] wesernetz's information page still tells households to register plug-in devices with it.[37] Each costs most for a household without the money or contacts to hand the job to a firm. This study does not measure their effect.

### 5.7 Grid capacity: not testable

wesernetz reports no congestion,[33] and nobody publishes capacity by neighbourhood (section 2.7). No evidence links grid capacity to the pattern in Part II, and no public data can rule out limits on individual low-voltage lines.

### 5.8 What follows

These are inferences from the findings, not evaluated policies.

A means-tested plug-in grant is the only instrument that reaches the low-income tenants of the most-rented postcodes quickly, now that federal law lets a tenant demand the landlord's consent.[18] The state parliament voted for such a grant in 2022 and the Senate has not delivered it.[25][26]

For the number of households reached, the larger lever in the inner city and central Bremerhaven is the roofs of blocks owned by GEWOBA, BREBAU and owners' associations. What holds those roofs back is metering, administration and the building code (section 5.5), not payback.

If the 2027 bill passes as drafted, new rooftop systems under 25 kW largely lose their feed-in support.[12] In my illustration the payback for a late adopter lengthens from about 12 years to about 18, and the late adopters are disproportionately in the postcodes that are behind today.

Bremen's most-rented postcodes are its postcodes of flats in blocks, and the most-rented fifth has 8.8 plug-in PV systems per 1,000 households. What stops the next household in Neustadt: the landlord, the €400, or the absence of anyone on the same staircase who has done it?
