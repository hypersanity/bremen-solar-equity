# House style guide (excerpt)

*v0.3, written by the human user before this study. This excerpt keeps the writing rules the report was rewritten to. Sections specific to a separate magazine project have been removed: personas and bylines, internal cross-references, and a worked example.*

## The voice in one sentence

Plain words carrying an unplain thought: **a counter-intuitive causal claim,
evidenced by one concrete thing, held open at the end.**

---

## The seven moves

1. **Lead with the claim, not the context.** The first sentence states something. If it sets a scene, it is the wrong first sentence.
2. **One idea per sentence; one claim per paragraph.** The Economist's test for a coherent piece: it "will suffer if even one sentence is cut out".
3. **Evidence by instance.** A named place, a number, an object, a date. Show the orange vest, not the concept of visibility.
4. **Implicate the writer.** Where the argument has a beneficiary — and most arguments about power do — say who it is, including when it is us.
5. **Test every framework.** State what the theory predicts, then what the case does instead. Theory is a hypothesis with a track record, not an authority.
6. **Write the blunt summary.** One flagged paragraph stating the whole argument in plain words. It is the claim.
7. **End on the gap.** Not a summary, not a conclusion — the next question, on the same thread.

**Terminology: repeat it.** State a key term once, then use one short fixed name
for the rest of the piece. Repetition of *content* is banned; repetition of
*terminology* is required. The opposite instinct — reaching for a fresh synonym
each time — is the documented machine fault called elegant variation.

## The bans

| Banned | Reason |
|---|---|
| *revolutionary · paradigm shift · unprecedented · game-changer* | Hype incurs a debt the analysis must then repay. |
| *insofar as · with regard to · in terms of · the fact that* | Nominal padding. Say the thing. |
| *it is important to note · arguably · one might argue* | Announces thinking instead of doing it. |
| *moving forward · noted, · to be fair,* as openers | Throat-clearing with punctuation. |
| More than one hedge per claim | Two hedges make the sentence a shrug. |
| Two abstract nouns or more in a single clause | Name an actor and give it a verb instead. |
| The passive where an actor exists | "It was decided" hides who decided. Orwell's rule 4. |
| Metaphors used in print elsewhere — *elephant in the room · double-edged sword · tip of the iceberg* | Orwell's rule 1. A dead metaphor imports someone else's thought. |
| Any metaphor that then needs a footnote, a gloss, or an "in other words" | If it must be explained, cut it and say the literal thing. |
| Any word needing a footnote to defend | Cut the word. |

The canon behind these is Orwell's six rules (1946), reproduced verbatim as the
base of the Economist guide. The sixth is the important one: *break any of these
rules sooner than say anything outright barbarous.* This is a discipline, not a
cage.

## Openings and endings

**Openings: thesis first, then motion.** The Economist's model is Bacon: "Of
Riches" begins "I cannot call riches better than the baggage of virtue" — a whole
essay implicit in one line.

**Endings: open a gap, do not close one.** Loewenstein (1994): curiosity requires a
priming dose of knowledge and then a felt gap. A summary closes the gap; a
near-miss question on the same thread opens the next piece.

## The de-slop pass

The default register of machine writing has documented tics. Run a **separate
de-slop pass at the end**, on the finished text, ideally by a reader that did not
draft it. Check for:

| # | Pattern | The tell |
|---|---------|----------|
| 1 | Significance inflation | *stands as · is a testament to · a pivotal moment · underscores its importance · reflects broader* |
| 2 | Promotional register | *vibrant · boasts · nestled · in the heart of · must-visit* |
| 3 | Vague attribution | *experts argue · observers have noted · industry reports* with nobody named |
| 4 | Superficial `-ing` tails | *highlighting, underscoring, reflecting, contributing to, showcasing* tacked onto a sentence for fake depth |
| 5 | Copula avoidance | *serves as · stands as · represents* where *is* would do |
| 6 | Excessive hedging | stacked qualifiers in one clause |
| 7 | Signposting | *let's dive in · here's what you need to know* — announcing the work instead of doing it |
| 8 | Fragmented headers | a heading followed by a one-line paragraph that merely restates the heading |
| 9 | Forced metaphors | an invented metaphor that is then explained; if it needs the explanation, cut the metaphor |
| 10 | Rule of three | ideas forced into triples to look comprehensive |
| 11 | Elegant variation | synonym cycling to avoid repeating a term |
| 12 | Generic positive conclusion | *the future looks bright · exciting times lie ahead* |
| 13 | Dramatic fragmentation | subjectless two-word sentences and a quotable line ending every section |
| 14 | Reassurance kickers | *and that's okay · there's nothing wrong with that* — trust the reader and stop |

**Targets beat prohibitions.** The most reliable way to avoid a banned pattern is
not to forbid it but to supply what replaces it: use the seven moves.

## The mechanical layer

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| Spelling | British throughout: *-ise*, not *-ize*; *judgement*; *programme* (but *program* for software) |
| Numbers | Figures for 10 and above, words below; never start a sentence with a numeral |
| Percentages | Percentage points for differences between percentages; never "a 5% increase" for 43%→48% |
| Money | Currency then figure (*€1,240*), or spell the currency; state the year of a nominal figure |
| Dates | *14 November 2024*, no ordinal suffix, no comma; **never a month range without a year** |
| Names | Checked against the source at first use, then copied exactly |
| Citations | Harvard. Every in-text citation reconciled against the reference list as a **separate mechanical pass** — never as part of writing |
| Placeholders | None survive to a finished draft |
| Abbreviations | Full form at first mention, short form thereafter; do not spatter capitals |
| Italics | Foreign words, publication and book titles |
| Quotation | Verbatim, including awkwardness; ellipsis in square brackets for cuts |
| Tense | One tense per section. Dates carry years |
| Dashes | Em dash — spaced or unspaced, but consistently within a piece. **No more than one per 150 words of body text.** |

## Pre-flight checklist

1. Does the first sentence *state* anything, or clear its throat?
2. Can I name the counter-intuitive claim in one line? If not, there isn't one yet.
3. Is one concrete object, number or place carrying the argument?
4. Hedges counted: one per claim, maximum?
5. Abstract nouns: no more than two per clause?
6. Any passive where the actor is known?
7. Any metaphor needing a footnote or an "in other words"?
8. Is there a blunt summary, and does it stand up on its own?
9. Names, dates and figures checked against the source *and* the reference list, not memory?
10. Any placeholder left in, duplicated word, or date missing its year?
11. Em dashes counted against the one-per-150-words ceiling?
12. Terminology repeated consistently, with no synonym cycling?
13. Read aloud once — does anything catch?
14. Has the de-slop pass been run by a reader who did not draft it?
15. Does the ending open a question on the same thread rather than summarise?
16. Would the piece suffer if any single sentence were cut? (The Economist's test.)
